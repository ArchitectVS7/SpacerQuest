## The pitch, in one line

> **FTL × Taipan × Citizen Sleeper, set in a galaxy full of D&D characters who
> play the game whether you're watching or not.** (PRD §1)

*Spacer Quest: Rimward* is a single-player space trading game with a fully
committed retro-terminal aesthetic, built on the bones of the 1991 Apple II BBS
game *Spacer Quest* — its star systems, factions, characters, and economy — but
redesigned from first principles for how people actually play in 2026.

## Three ideas carry the game (PRD §1)

1. **Everything runs on the d20.** Each morning you roll a hand of dice — that's
   your day's luck, visible up front. You decide where the good rolls go: the
   risky smuggling run or the getaway you might need later. Every other spacer in
   the galaxy runs on the same dice, the same stats, and the same capacity for
   bad judgment.
2. **The galaxy is a cast, not a backdrop.** Thirty-plus named NPCs each carry a
   full character sheet: stats, a backstory, an Ideal, a Bond, and a Flaw that
   mechanically drives them to make *interesting* choices instead of optimal
   ones. They trade, feud, gamble, get arrested, and take the contracts you turn
   down. The news wire reports what they did while you slept.
3. **Trading carries story.** Cargo isn't a price delta — it's a decision with a
   face on it. Medicine for a plague station, a passenger who lies about her
   name, contraband a patrol captain will remember you for.

## What makes it different (PRD §4, condensed)

- **A d20 society, not an AI backdrop.** No competitor in the trading genre runs
  its entire NPC population on transparent tabletop-RPG logic — sheets, rolls and
  flaws the player can learn and exploit.
- **The dice are honest and visible.** The morning roll shows your luck before
  you spend it. The game never fudges; tension comes from allocation, not hidden
  odds.
- **Fuel is the plot.** One resource gates travel, combat and escape. "Can I
  afford to fire my guns this far from a depot?" — a question no other trading
  game asks on every encounter. (Discovered, not invented: automated playtesting
  of the restored 1991 rules surfaced it as the most interesting tension there.)
- **Terminal aesthetic as instrument, not wallpaper.** One screen — a diegetic
  ship's computer: starmap, manifest, fuel gauge, news wire — in committed
  amber-phosphor CRT with modern typography, animation and sound. Duskers-grade
  commitment, not a scanline shader on a menu.
- **A living lineage.** A real 1991 BBS game, rediscovered and reimagined by
  someone who waited thirty years to play it. The authentic roster, systems,
  factions and formulas survive inside a modern design.
- **Premium and finished, with room to grow.** No microtransactions, no energy
  timers, no season passes. Expansions are content.

## The first session — Tour One (PRD §5.1)

You are a nobody with a junker ship and a 25,000-credit debt to the Merchant
Guild of Sun-3, due in 30 days. The object is stated on the first screen: **clear
the debt and make your name.** Tour One is a complete authored arc of **60–120
minutes** that doubles as the tutorial, ending on a decisive Day-30 beat and a
hook — a fragment of a signal that shouldn't exist — that opens the veteran
career.

**The demo is Tour One**, shipped as a first-class build configuration with its
own Steam app. It is the whole first arc, and a career carried out of it can be
imported into the full game.

## Beyond it — the veteran loop (PRD §5.2)

Climb the Registry of Deeds through nine ranks to **Conqueror**; commit to one of
four alliance arcs and change how the other three treat you; chase the Signal
across a whole career toward a one-way crossing to Andromeda. **Death is legacy,
not game over:** your successor inherits your charts, half your bank, your Deeds
and every grudge your name earned.

## The 1991 hook (PRD §4.5)

*Spacer Quest* v3.4 by Firefox ran on Apple II GBBS boards in 1991. Its source
was located, decompiled and faithfully reimplemented in 2026, then validated by
an automated playtest harness to roughly 1,500 actions. The conclusion — that the
1991 *design* is not fun in 2026 but its *systems* are worth a real game — is
what *Rimward* is. That story is the marketing hook no competitor can copy.
